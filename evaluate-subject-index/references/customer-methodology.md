# How the Subject Index Evaluation Works

This evaluation measures how well a finished subject index helps readers find and understand the substantive content of a specific publication. It is source-grounded: the index is judged against the publication itself, not against another index and not against a list of terms copied from the candidate.

## What customers see

Results are presented in four layers so that a reader can understand the conclusion without working through the complete audit.

| Layer | What it answers | Typical content |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Overall result | How strong is this index as delivered? | Total score, interpretation, publication-readiness status, and a short conclusion |
| 2. Six quality questions | Where is it strong or weak? | Six dimension scores, plain-language explanations, and density calibration |
| 3. Supporting evidence | Why did it receive those scores? | Measured rates, representative strengths and defects, chapter patterns, and failed checks |
| 4. Full methodology | Exactly what was tested? | Scope, policy, item-level judgments, denominators, limitations, and technical artifacts |

The first two layers are designed for customers and decision-makers. The deeper layers make the result inspectable without crowding the main presentation.

## How the evaluation works

The process has five main stages.

### 1. Establish the source and scope

The evaluator confirms the exact publication and edition, maps the page labels used by the book to pages in the supplied file, and records which parts of the source are eligible for indexing. Chapters or other intellectual units are assigned explicit page ranges.

The normal scope includes substantive body text and inspectable substantive notes, captions, and table text. Bibliographies, navigation pages, running furniture, proof material, and any publisher or candidate index are excluded.

### 2. Determine what the source substantively treats

Before the candidate index is inspected, the source is read chapter by chapter to identify its important subjects, arguments, findings, relationships, distinctions, causes, consequences, and other treatments a reader may reasonably need to find.

This is not keyword extraction. A concept can matter even if it appears only once, and a frequently repeated name or term may be unimportant. Each subject is recorded with its meaning, importance, source evidence, relevant pages, distinctions, and plausible reader access routes.

### 3. Freeze a source-based checklist

The chapter findings are reconciled into a publication-level source benchmark: a frozen, candidate-blind checklist of substantive reader needs. It describes what the source treats and how readers might reasonably look for it; it does not prescribe one ideal wording or index structure.

Freezing the benchmark before opening the candidate prevents the circular method of discovering concepts from the index and then crediting the same index for containing them.

### 4. Audit the candidate in both directions

The candidate is normalized without silently correcting it. Displayed page ranges are expanded into individual path-and-page assignments, and every assignment is mapped back to the source.

The audit then runs in two directions:

- **Index to source:** Does each complete heading path accurately point to substantive treatment on every page it cites?
- **Source to index:** Can readers find the important subjects and treatment pages identified independently from the source?

These passes measure different things. The first detects unsupported or misleading access; the second detects omissions and missed treatment.

### 5. Review the index as a complete navigation system

After the page-level audit, the evaluator judges the whole index: terminology, hierarchy, subdivisions, alternative access, cross-references, fragmentation, consistency, distribution, density, and mechanics. The final score is calculated from the evidence ledgers, and critical publication-readiness checks are applied separately.

## What the score means

The evaluation produces six dimension scores totaling 100 points.

| Customer question | Dimension | Weight |
| --- | --- | ---: |
| Does it include what matters? | Meaningful coverage | 20 |
| Does it leave out what does not belong? | Editorial selectivity, including density fit | 15 |
| Does it represent the book accurately? | Conceptual and stance fidelity | 15 |
| Does it point to the right pages? | Page-reference reliability | 25 |
| Can readers find things efficiently? | Findability and navigation | 20 |
| Is it professionally consistent? | Mechanics and consistency | 5 |

The total is a weighted quality summary, not “percent correct.” Each dimension is based on measured results and item-level evidence. The report publishes the component scores and supporting rates so that the total can be interpreted rather than taken on faith.

| Total | Interpretation |
| ---: | --- |
| 90–100 | Excellent finished index; potentially near publication-ready after full review |
| 80–89 | Strong index; meaningful editorial review still needed |
| 70–79 | Useful foundation; substantial revision required |
| 60–69 | Weak finished index; major reconstruction required |
| Below 60 | Poor reader access; not suitable as delivered |

A high total does not by itself mean publication-ready. A critical failure can limit that claim even when most of the index is strong.

## What the six scores evaluate

### 1. Meaningful coverage — 20 points

Measures whether readers can find the publication’s principal subjects, important subsidiary subjects, arguments, findings, relationships, distinctions, and conclusions. It considers both concept coverage and whether the important treatment pages are represented.

### 2. Editorial selectivity — 15 points

Measures whether access points reflect substantive treatment rather than passing mentions, citation-only names, incidental examples, or redundant clutter. Ten points assess substantive selectivity; up to five points assess whether density is reasonably fitted to the source.

### 3. Conceptual and stance fidelity — 15 points

Measures whether headings say what the source actually says. It tests meaning, scope, distinctions, chronology, and the difference between claims that the author supports, proposes, questions, qualifies, rejects, or merely reports.

### 4. Page-reference reliability — 25 points

Measures whether locators point to pages that substantively support the complete heading path and whether the index includes the principal pages needed for that subject. Each page in a displayed range is tested individually.

### 5. Findability and navigation — 20 points

Measures whether readers can locate subjects efficiently through clear terminology, useful direct access, meaningful subdivisions, accurate parent-child relationships, valid cross-references, and coherent organization across the entire book.

### 6. Mechanics and consistency — 5 points

Measures professional execution: valid and ordered locators, unique heading paths, consistent names and wording, correct reference types and targets, no empty entries, no impermissible third-level headings, and conformance to the required data structure.

## How index density is assessed

The framework uses two standardized chapter-level calibration targets:

- **8 locator-bearing complete heading paths per 1,000 indexable source words**
- **20 expanded locator occurrences per 1,000 indexable source words**

The normal target bands are 6–10 paths and 15–25 locator occurrences. Broad tolerance bands of 4–12 paths and 10–30 locator occurrences allow substantial variation before density is treated as a serious defect.

These figures are calibration points, not quotas, minimums, hard ceilings, or universal professional requirements. A source may justify natural variation. Valid access is not removed to reach a number, and weak access is not retained to fill one.

Density is measured by chapter because a whole-book average can hide overindexed and underindexed sections. Chapter results are weighted by indexable source words. Density can contribute no more than five points to the 100-point score; specific omissions, clutter, or navigation failures must still be demonstrated from source evidence.

For a 10,000-word chapter, the calibration is approximately 80 locator-bearing heading paths and 200 locator occurrences. The report shows the observed chapter distribution as well as the aggregate fit.

## Publication-readiness checks

Critical checks are evaluated separately from the score. An applicable failed check prevents an unqualified “publication-ready” conclusion. Checks include:

- fabricated, nonexistent, or out-of-scope locators;
- a systematic pattern of incidental or unsupported locators;
- a materially omitted central subject or conclusion;
- a central claim that is fabricated, reversed, or seriously misrepresented;
- a compound heading whose cited pages support only separate components;
- a `see` reference that replaces a warranted substantive entry;
- unresolved, self-referential, circular, or chained cross-references;
- any third-level heading;
- systematic named-entity, example, or citation clutter;
- critical or major unresolved source grounding;
- excessive uninspectable locator assignments under the declared tolerance;
- the wrong source span; or
- structurally invalid or incomplete output.

A failed check does not secretly change the arithmetic score. The report shows the check, its status, and its supporting evidence.

## Why the method is fair and auditable

- **Source-first:** Important subjects are identified from the publication before the candidate is reviewed.
- **Candidate-blind benchmark:** The candidate does not define the concepts used to measure its own coverage.
- **No index is the gold standard:** A publisher’s index or competing index is not assumed to be correct.
- **Independent evaluation:** Each candidate is audited separately. Scores can be placed side by side only when the source, benchmark, policy, page map, chunk design, audit mode, and uncertainty rules match.
- **Two-way testing:** The method tests both unsupported access and missing access.
- **Atomic evidence:** Locator judgments are made on one complete heading path and one source page at a time.
- **Traceable scoring:** Scores are derived from recorded judgments, denominators, and calculation rules rather than overall impressions.
- **Frozen inputs:** The policy, benchmark, and comparison identifiers are versioned and hashed so later changes are visible.
- **Recorded uncertainty:** Uninspectable or uncertain material is disclosed rather than silently counted as success or failure.

The workflow combines deterministic processing with editorial judgment. Page mapping, range expansion, hashing, schema validation, density arithmetic, and score arithmetic are deterministic. Decisions about substantive significance, conceptual accuracy, and navigation quality require source-grounded editorial judgment and therefore include evidence and confidence.

## Detailed evaluation criteria

Expand any category for the detailed questions applied during the audit.

<details>
<summary><strong>Scope compliance</strong></summary>

- Is only approved, indexable source matter used?
- Are substantive body text, inspectable notes, captions, quotations, and tables handled when eligible?
- Are bibliographies, navigation lists, running furniture, proof material, excluded back matter, and other indexes kept out?
- On mixed pages, is eligible content distinguished from excluded regions?
- Are unavailable portions disclosed rather than treated as omissions without evidence?

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Substantive coverage</strong></summary>

- Are principal and independently useful subsidiary subjects findable?
- Are arguments, findings, causes, consequences, comparisons, distinctions, methods, applications, synthesis, and conclusions represented?
- Are important one-off concepts preserved even when they are not frequent terms?
- Is coverage distributed by intellectual importance rather than equally by chapter or word count?
- Do readers have access to the principal, supporting, and conclusion-level treatment pages?

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Editorial selectivity</strong></summary>

- Does every locator-bearing path represent substantive treatment?
- Are passing mentions, attribution-only scholars, citation-only names, and incidental entities excluded?
- Are examples and evidence indexed independently only when sustained, analytically important, or useful to retrieve?
- Does the index avoid concordance-like repetition, redundant access, and trivial subdivisions?
- Is chapter-level density within a defensible range for the material?

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Conceptual and stance fidelity</strong></summary>

- Does every heading accurately describe the source?
- Are supported, proposed, possible, questioned, qualified, rejected, inconclusive, and reported claims distinguished?
- Are materially different subjects, periods, senses, scopes, corpora, explanations, and findings kept distinct?
- Do broad or compound headings avoid claiming more than every cited page supports?
- Are specialist names, notation, transliteration, diacritics, and capitalization accurate?

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Heading and access architecture</strong></summary>

- Are headings concise, specific, reader-oriented, and independently searchable?
- Are true synonyms consolidated and materially different senses distinguished?
- Does each path contain one main heading and no more than one subheading?
- Do subdivisions express meaningful aspects or reader questions rather than page, section, or grammatical divisions?
- Are important concepts given intuitive direct or cross-referenced access instead of being buried beneath vague umbrellas?
- Do locator-bearing parents contain real general treatment rather than merely duplicating their children?

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Locator quality</strong></summary>

- Does each page substantively support the complete heading path?
- Are incidental mentions, citations, isolated examples, and merely encompassing statements excluded?
- Are all principal and relevant continuation, comparison, synthesis, and conclusion pages included?
- Is every page in a displayed range evaluated independently?
- Are locators valid, unique, in publication order, and resolved to indexable source pages?
- Does every final locator have exact source evidence or an explicit unresolved status?

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Compound-heading scope</strong></summary>

- Does every locator support every component asserted by a compound or enumerated heading?
- Have component-specific locators been improperly combined under one formulation?
- Where needed, are distinct treatments split without losing stance, chronology, corpus, finding, or explanation?
- If a common umbrella is used, is it both accurate and useful rather than vague?

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Cross-references</strong></summary>

- Does each `see` reference represent a plausible synonym, variant, alternative name, or useful inversion?
- Does a `see` source correctly have no locators or locator-bearing descendants?
- Does each `see also` connect distinct, independently indexed subjects in a useful way?
- Does every target resolve exactly?
- Are self-references, cycles, chained `see` references, misleading links, and excessive reference webs absent?
- Do references improve navigation rather than conceal fragmented organization?

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Whole-index coherence</strong></summary>

- Is one reader concept kept from fragmenting among competing headings or schemes?
- Are parallel subjects organized and worded in reasonably parallel ways?
- Are terminology, capitalization, hierarchy, and sibling wording consistent?
- Does the index balance useful specificity with coherent grouping?
- Is coverage proportionate, without repeated terminology, names, or examples crowding out central subjects?
- Does the result work as one publication-level navigation system rather than a set of concatenated chapter indexes?

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Mechanical validity</strong></summary>

- Does every page-bearing entry have one unique one- or two-level path and at least one valid locator?
- Are duplicate paths merged and locator lists deduplicated and ordered?
- Are only `see` and `see also` used, with valid and deduplicated targets?
- Are empty entries, invalid records, and third-level headings absent?
- Does the complete result conform to the required data schema?

</details>

The complete normative rules, scoring formulas, error codes, and stage assignments are available in the [standard policy](standard-policy.md), [rubric](rubric.md), and [judgment policy](judgment-policy.md).

## What customers receive

A completed web report should include:

- an overall score, interpretation, and restrained source-specific conclusion;
- publication-readiness check results;
- the six dimension scores and plain-language explanations;
- measured precision, recall, coverage, navigation, and mechanics results with denominators;
- a density card showing the framework targets, chapter distribution, fit, and limited score contribution;
- representative strengths and the most consequential defects;
- expandable evidence examples tied to exact audit records;
- the audited source/candidate identity, scope, audit mode, and comparison key;
- relevant limitations, uncertainty, and uninspectable material; and
- a machine-readable JSON result for web presentation or independent checking.

## How individual index elements are graded

In addition to the publication-level score, the evaluation produces diagnostic grades for individual locators, complete heading paths, displayed main headings and subheadings, cross-references, and independently discovered source subjects. These grades make it possible to display the candidate index with semantic color coding and to inspect any element through a hover, keyboard-focus, or touch popover.

The grades answer different questions:

- A locator grade asks whether one complete heading path is substantively supported on one cited page.
- A complete-path grade summarizes the measured quality of an entry as delivered, including its locator and path-specific evidence.
- A heading-node grade evaluates the wording and structural role of the displayed main heading or subheading; it does not hide a weak child by averaging descendants.
- A cross-reference grade asks whether one `see` or `see also` reference is valid and useful.
- A source-subject grade identifies complete, partial, or missing access to a subject discovered independently from the source.

Each popover shows the element’s grade, the factors that affected it, applicable weights or severity caps, confidence, a concise explanation, and links to evidence IDs. Public popovers use short paraphrases rather than long source quotations. “Not measured” and “uninspectable” are shown neutrally rather than colored as failures.

Individual grades are diagnostic. They are not added together to calculate the overall 100-point score, and chapter density is never assigned to a particular heading. See the [item-grading specification](item-grading.md) for the exact rules.

Full audits inspect all in-scope source pages, delivered index records, expanded locator assignments, scored benchmark subjects, cross-references, and structural flags. A pilot uses a disclosed sample to calibrate the method and cannot support full-index claims.

## Limits and interpretation

- The score applies only to the identified source edition and candidate version.
- Editorial judgments are evidence-based but are not perfectly deterministic; another qualified evaluation may differ at the margins.
- The frozen policy, benchmark, denominators, and item-level evidence make disagreements inspectable and reruns meaningfully comparable.
- Density targets belong to this framework and are not claimed as universal indexing standards.
- A comparison is valid only when candidates were evaluated under compatible frozen inputs and the same audit mode.
- Copyrighted source passages should be shown only to the extent needed to explain a judgment.

## Terminology

<details>
<summary><strong>Source benchmark</strong></summary>

The candidate-blind, source-based checklist of substantive concepts, treatment pages, distinctions, and reader tasks. It is frozen before candidate review.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Complete heading path</strong></summary>

The full access statement formed by a main heading and, when present, its subheading. Locator support is tested against the complete path, not against isolated words.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Locator-bearing heading path</strong></summary>

One unique complete heading path that has at least one page reference. It counts once per chapter in which it has one or more locator assignments for density measurement.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Locator occurrence</strong></summary>

One normalized complete-heading-path and source-page assignment. A displayed range is expanded and each page counts as a separate occurrence.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Reader task</strong></summary>

A realistic attempt to find a substantive subject using terminology a reader might plausibly choose. Reader-task success tests practical access, not only exact heading matches.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Publication-readiness check</strong></summary>

A critical condition assessed separately from the weighted score. A failed applicable check prevents an unqualified publication-ready conclusion.

</details>
